# Voice and genre

The person's voice file is the main evidence of their voice: its *Voice*, *My rules*, *My terms* and *Fingerprint* sections, the *Examples* in each mode, and both kinds of *Learned* entries. Samples they paste during a session add to it; they don't replace it. Where the voice file and a general rule disagree on style, the voice file wins.

Two or three unassisted samples in a similar genre are more useful than a long list of adjectives. This is an editorial heuristic, not a validated minimum. A short sample supports only a tentative match.

Read for what the writer notices: where they begin, how directly they claim something, which details they explain, how they express doubt, and whether humour or first person is normal. Voice is more than punctuation counts.

If there is no voice file yet, build a small working profile for this task: audience relationship, stance, level of detail, vocabulary and dialect, rhythm, habits to retain, and habits to avoid. Use evidenced features without quotas. Do not transplant personal facts or distinctive phrases from a sample into a different text.

Treat any profile as a working hypothesis. Distinguish features observed in multiple samples from tentative impressions or explicit preferences. Let the person's corrections override inferred style. Do not claim to have recovered a complete personal voice. Save to the voice file only with the person's permission, as SKILL.md describes under "Learn only with permission".

A raw speech transcript can reveal how the author explains ideas and expresses doubt. Its repetitions, punctuation and false starts may come from speech or transcription rather than a writing preference. Remove them in an edited written account when appropriate; preserve them if a verbatim transcript is requested. Do not infer vocal pitch, accent or delivery from text alone.

Natural Voice writes in English only. Take the spelling variant from the voice file (*Settings*, "Spelling"). If the voice file is silent, follow the person's samples or the text you are editing. Use British English only as a last resort, when nothing else tells you which variety to use. A request for one piece ("American spelling for this client") wins for that piece. A general request to sound professional doesn't change the variety.

Adapt style to the intended audience without silently changing cultural references, stance, politeness or dialect.

| Genre | Make it work by | Preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Email or work message | Put purpose and action early; sound like a colleague | Relationship, deadline, commitments, urgency |
| CV or cover letter | Connect supported experience to the role | Official titles, dates, ownership, outcomes, relevant terms |
| Product case study | Explain problem, decision, constraint, and evidence | Research vs assumption, proposed vs shipped, contribution vs leadership |
| Academic or technical prose | Make arguments and definitions precise | Citations, notation, methods, limitations, necessary hedging |
| Policy or regulated information | Explain conditions clearly | Must/may/should, warnings, eligibility, exclusions |
| UI copy | Help someone decide or act | Labels, state, next action, accessibility, length constraints |
| Article or essay | Develop a particular supported argument | Actual stance, sources, nuance |
| Fiction | Follow narrative perspective and creative brief | Character continuity, tone, deliberate devices |
| English with words from other languages | Keep borrowed words, names and quotations as written; don't translate them | Spelling, accents, script, meaning |

Natural technical prose can be formal. Human writing can be symmetrical, grammatical, and structured. The explicit brief wins over a sample from a different genre.

## Original example

Supplied claim: “The prototype displays the annual charge of 0.75% on the Review screen. It has not been usability tested.”

Formulaic draft: “By leveraging a seamless and transparent experience, the prototype empowers users with unparalleled clarity. The annual charge of 0.75% is displayed on the Review screen. The prototype has not been usability tested.”

Revision: “The prototype shows the 0.75% annual charge on the Review screen. It has not been usability tested.”

The revision retains the design and limitation without claiming that users understood the charge. This is an editorial example, not a detector-tested result.
