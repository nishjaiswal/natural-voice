# From how people talk to what they mean

People who dictate know exactly what they mean, but the route there loops. Your job is to find where they end up, not to tidy the route. These patterns are common to almost everyone who thinks out loud. The person's own profile (the voice file's *How I talk* section) adds their personal ones.

## Common patterns

1. **The point often comes last.** People build up with context, then land it with phrases like "so basically", "what I'm saying is", "the idea is" or "in the end". Read to the end before deciding what the point is.
2. **When they correct themselves, the last version wins.** "A tool, or no, a template, actually a checklist" means **a checklist**. If two versions genuinely conflict, ask.
3. **Thinking noises and hedges.** "like", "you know", "basically", "right" and "um" are thinking out loud: drop them. "I think", "I guess", "probably", "kind of" and "sort of" are sometimes noise and sometimes a real hedge. Drop them only when they carry no meaning ("I think we shipped it in May", when they know they did). Keep the uncertainty, in plain words, when they sit on a judgement, a cause, an observation about people or an amount: "I think people just didn't get it" becomes "it looked like people didn't get it", and "I think about 30%" becomes "about 30%" plus a question. When in doubt, ask in the read-back. Dropping a hedge is upgrading a claim.
4. **Describing a thing by what it does.** People say "the big board where you can drag stuff around" when they mean an interactive canvas. Map it to the right term using the glossary. Behaviour descriptions often make the best captions, so keep the concrete actions.
5. **Explaining through an example.** "For example, say you want to..." Usually the example *is* the content. Keep the concrete detail and drop the framing.
6. **Saying the same thing three ways.** That is emphasis, not three facts. Say it once.
7. **Starting in the middle.** People often jump to what they did and add why later. Put it back in the order the chosen mode needs.
8. **Everyday words for big ideas.** "Make it less scary" might mean build confidence. "People just left" might mean drop-off. Use the plainest accurate term the person's samples would use. Don't jump to jargon.
9. **Dictation mistakes.** Names, products and tools get misheard, and punctuation is missing or random. If a word makes no sense and you can't infer it confidently, ask.

## Method

1. Read the whole thing once.
2. Pick out every concrete fact: numbers, things on screen, people, actions, observations.
3. Find the landing sentence (pattern 1) and the final version of any correction (pattern 2).
4. Decide which job this piece does in the chosen mode, for example problem, decision, result, step or caption.
5. Write a one-sentence read-back the person could have written themselves on a good day.
6. Everything else either supports that sentence or gets cut.

## Never

- Never polish away the person's opinions. Their judgements are part of their voice.
- Never add reasons they didn't give. If the "why" matters and is missing, ask.
- Never upgrade a claim.
